### Revising the question after a preliminary search

Appendix B provides space for an updated question. **Chapter 7, “Evidence Phase: Searching for Evidence” (especially p. 95),** explains how to isolate the population, setting, topic or intervention, and possible outcome for a search. **Appendix C, “Searching and Screening Tool” (beginning p. 218),** transfers those elements from the Question Development Tool into the evidence-search plan. These sections are useful when the first search shows that the question needs to be focused, broadened, or reworded.

**Suggested reading order:** Appendix A → Chapter 3 → Chapter 4 → Appendix B. Use Chapter 2 for an overview; consult Chapter 7 and Appendix C when testing or revising the question through a preliminary evidence search.

### Process for Completing Appendix B

*Johns Hopkins Evidence-Based Practice for Nurses and Healthcare Professionals: Model & Guidelines*, fifth edition (2025)

The eight steps below are a practical workflow drawn from the textbook; they are not numbered this way in the book. Page numbers refer to the **printed textbook pages**.

| Step | Appendix B field | What to write | Where to read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Describe the local problem** | “What is the local problem?” | Write brief bullets or phrases naming the population, the specific issue observed, and where or when it occurs. Describe the gap between what is happening and what should happen. If the underlying issue is uncertain, record the observation without claiming an unverified cause. Example: “Adults discharged from [unit] report difficulty understanding [care instructions].” | **Chapter 3, pp. 44–45; Appendix B, pp. 212, 214.** |
| **2. Explain why it matters** | “Why is this problem important and relevant? What would happen if it was not addressed?” | Identify the people affected, the possible clinical, safety, experience, staff, or operational consequences, and why the issue warrants attention now. Explain what is likely to persist or worsen if practice stays the same; distinguish measured effects from plausible concerns. Example: “Unclear instructions may interfere with [self-care task] and increase [identified risk].” | **Chapter 3, p. 51; Appendix B, pp. 212, 214.** |
| **3. Document current practice and local data** | “What is the current practice in the EBP team’s setting?” and “What data from the EBP team’s setting indicates there is a problem?” | For **current practice**, describe the usual workflow, applicable policy, what staff actually do, and any observed difference between policy and practice. For **local data**, give the source, measurement period, denominator where relevant, result, and benchmark or prior value if available. Suitable evidence can include quality and safety measures, audits, patient or staff reports, practice observations, financial information, or a documented lack of evidence for current practice. Example: “During [period], [number]/[denominator] discharge records met [measure], compared with [target].” If data are unavailable, say so and name what must be collected; do not invent a rate. | **Chapter 3, pp. 44, 51; Appendix B, pp. 212, 214.** |
| **4. Write the problem statement** | “Considering all of the information above, create a concise problem statement below.” | Combine the verified points above into **one or two sentences** identifying the population, topic or practice gap, impact, and local context or magnitude when known. State the problem without choosing a solution or assigning an unverified cause. Example: “Among [population] in [setting], [documented practice gap] affects [outcome or experience], as shown by [local measure].” | **Chapter 3, pp. 47–48, 51; Appendix B, pp. 212, 214.** |
| **5. Choose broad or intervention** | “Will this be a broad or intervention EBP question?” | Mark **Broad** if you need to explore a range of best practices for a topic, especially when available approaches are not yet clear. Mark **Intervention** if you have a specific intervention and want to assess its effect on a defined outcome; a comparison may be included. An initial broad evidence review may lead to an intervention question. This field requires a selection, not a paragraph; keep a brief rationale in team notes if useful. | **Chapter 4, pp. 58–60, 62; Appendix B, pp. 212, 214.** |
| **6. Identify question elements** | “Identify the relevant elements of the EBP question.” | Fill **Population** with the group and characteristics that define its relevance; **Setting** with the care environment; and **Topic** (broad) *or* **Intervention(s)** (intervention) with the issue or precise approach under review. For an intervention question, name a measurable **Outcome** and any second intervention being compared. Leave elements that do not apply unforced: broad questions do not specify an intervention or outcome. Use terms precise enough to guide searching without needlessly excluding relevant evidence. | **Chapter 4, pp. 59–60; Appendix B, pp. 213, 215.** |
| **7. Draft and check the EBP question** | “Record the completed EBP question below.” | Write **one complete, searchable question** using the matching broad or intervention sentence structure on Appendix B, p. 213. A broad question connects **[population and/or setting]** to best practices or strategies **regarding [topic]**. An intervention question asks for the **impact of [intervention] on [outcome]** in **[population and/or setting]**; when comparing interventions, name both. Check that the wording answers the problem statement, has a clear scope, and can direct an evidence search. Example broad: “Among [population] in [setting], what are best practices regarding [topic]?” Example intervention: “Among [population] in [setting], what is the impact of [intervention] on [outcome]?” | **Chapter 4, pp. 58–60; Appendix B, pp. 213, 215.** |
| **8. Search briefly and revise if needed** | “If needed after a preliminary evidence search/review, record an updated or revised EBP question here.” | Search preliminary internal and external evidence, then enter a **revised complete question** in the final field *if* the search shows that you should narrow or broaden the scope, use better terminology, or choose a different measure. Keep the original question in its original field so the change is visible. If the initial question remains suitable, there is no need to invent a revision; the form makes this field conditional. You may record the reason for a change in project notes. | **Chapter 4, p. 62; Appendix B, pp. 213, 215.** |

The most direct companion to this table is **Appendix B's field-by-field instructions on pp. 214–215**. Chapter 3 supports the problem-focused steps; Chapter 4 supports question construction and review. Before moving to the evidence phase, confirm that the local problem, importance, current practice, and supporting local data are each addressed in their own fields; the problem statement describes a problem rather than a proposed fix; the selected question type agrees with the elements and final question; and any revision follows an initial evidence review. Appendix B itself provides the field prompts on pp. 212–213 and the accompanying instructions on pp. 214–215.

### Process for completing Appendix C

The steps below are a practical way to work through the form; they are **not** a separately numbered textbook procedure. The targeted and exhaustive routes branch after Step 4.

| Step | Appendix C field or record | What to write | Where to read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Start with the completed EBP question** | Preparation for Appendix C; no separate question field on the form. | Bring forward the **final, current EBP question** from Appendix B, including any revision after preliminary review. Check that the question is focused enough to identify what evidence is relevant. Keep its wording beside Appendix C while searching and screening. The evidence-phase decision tree helps orient the team; this step itself does **not** call for a new paragraph on Appendix C. | **Chapter 2, pp. 29–30; Appendix A, pp. 208–209; Appendix B, pp. 212–215.** |
| **2. Complete Section I: key question elements** | **Population; Setting; Topic or Intervention(s); Outcomes (as needed).** | Transfer the group's defining characteristics to **Population** and the care environment to **Setting**. Enter the issue under **Topic** for a broad question, or name the specific **Intervention(s)** for an intervention question; list both interventions if the question compares them. Enter a specific outcome **only if the team plans to use it as a search concept**. For example: Population: `[group and defining features]`; Setting: `[care environment]`; Topic or Intervention(s): `[issue or approach]`; Outcomes: `[outcome, if used in the search]`. | **Chapter 4, pp. 59–60; Chapter 7, pp. 94–95; Appendix B, p. 213; Appendix C, pp. 218, 221.** |
| **3. Conduct the best-evidence search in Section II** | **“Does pre-appraised evidence exist ...?”** | Search for relevant **clinical practice guidelines, literature reviews with a systematic approach, and evidence summaries** that address the question. Check **Yes** if you found potentially relevant pre-appraised evidence and proceed to E1 appraisal; check **No** if you did not, and follow Section III. Keep a separate search log naming the source or site searched, date, terms, and candidate citation(s), because Section II gives a decision checkbox rather than a table for those details. Do not count an unscreened candidate as already suitable and adequate. | **Chapter 6, pp. 81–85; Chapter 7, pp. 95–97; Appendix C, pp. 218, 221.** |
| **4. Appraise pre-appraised evidence and choose a path** | Section II: **“Is the evidence suitable and of adequate quality?”** | For each candidate, complete **Appendix E1**: compare its population, setting, topic or intervention, outcomes, and **date its underlying search was conducted** with the team's question; assess the applicable quality items and record a reasoned inclusion or exclusion decision. Then check **Yes** in Section II only when suitable evidence of adequate quality can support the question, and follow Step 5. If the answer is **No**, follow Steps 6–9. Record appraisal details on E1 or in project notes; the Appendix C checkbox alone does not show the rationale. | **Chapter 8, pp. 122–126; Appendix A, p. 209; Appendix C, pp. 218, 221; Appendix E1, pp. 230–233.** |
| **5. If suitable pre-appraised evidence exists: conduct a targeted update search** | Section II **Yes → targeted search** branch; maintain a **separate targeted-search log**. | Note the **last search date inside the accepted pre-appraised source**, then search from that date onward for newer evidence relevant to the question, particularly evidence that may offer strong or moderate support for decision-making. In the separate log, write the source searched, date run, time window, exact search terms or string, result count, and which newer items were retained, with brief reasons. The form instructs the targeted search but has **no dedicated targeted-search results table**; Section III is labeled “Exhaustive Search and Screening.” Do not present a targeted search as exhaustive. | **Chapter 2, pp. 29–30; Chapter 7, p. 98; Appendix A, p. 209; Appendix C, pp. 218, 221.** |
| **6. Otherwise: plan Section III's exhaustive search** | **EBP Question Element / Possible Search Terms** table; **databases**; **inclusion and exclusion criteria**; **date limit and why**. | Put each useful question concept in a separate row and list its synonyms, alternate spellings, acronyms, subject headings, and brand names as appropriate. Add or remove rows if the number of concepts differs from the printed boxes. Do **not** search only for favorable directions such as “improve” or “reduce.” Select the databases suited to the topic and write any unlisted ones after **Other**. Under **Inclusion** and **Exclusion**, specify operational rules for population, setting, interventions or topic, evidence or publication type, language, and other relevant limits; say what belongs on each side. State the date range and a topic-specific reason for it; avoid an automatic five-year cutoff. Example: “Include `[defined population]` in `[setting]`; exclude `[distinct population or setting]`.” | **Chapter 7, pp. 98–105, 107, 110–111; Appendix C, pp. 218–219, 221–222.** |
| **7. Run, refine, and document the exhaustive search** | **Search date** and **Database / Search String / Number of Results** table. | Enter the **actual date the final search was run**. For **each database**, paste the exact final query as entered in that database, including database-specific subject headings, Boolean operators, field tags, and applied limits; enter the number of records it returned. Use **OR** between synonyms and **AND** between different concepts; adapt syntax to each database. If a trial search reveals missing terminology or unmanageable results, revise and rerun it, then record the **final** strategy in the form and preserve earlier versions and reasons in the separate search log. List the justification for any limits in the preceding Section III field. | **Chapter 7, pp. 105–108; Appendix C, pp. 219, 222.** |
| **8. Screen the exhaustive-search results systematically** | **“How will the team systematically screen the results ...?”** checkboxes, plus the working screening log. | Check the methods actually used: citation-tracking software or spreadsheet, **at least two independent reviewers for each record** if used, third-reviewer resolution if used, and an explanation after **Other** for any other method. In a separate screening log, record each citation, duplicate status, title/abstract decision, full-text decision, reason for exclusion, reviewers, and resolution of disagreements. Apply the same inclusion and exclusion rules to all records. Remove duplicate records first, then screen titles and abstracts, then evaluate the full texts that remain; do not mark a study excluded for inadequate methodological quality during eligibility screening when quality appraisal is a later step. | **Chapter 7, pp. 109–112; Appendix C, pp. 219–223.** |
| **9. Complete and reconcile the Section III flow chart** | **Total Number of Results; Number of Duplicates; Records Reviewed in Title and Abstract Screening / Records excluded; Records Reviewed in Full Text Screening / Records excluded; Records Included for Summary and Synthesis.** | Enter actual counts from the final database searches **plus hand searching**, then count overlapping records as duplicates. Record how many unique records entered title/abstract screening and how many were excluded there; record how many entered full-text screening and how many were excluded there; enter the remainder as included for later appraisal, summary, and synthesis. Check the arithmetic: **total − duplicates = title/abstract reviewed**; **title/abstract reviewed − title/abstract excluded = full texts reviewed**; **full texts reviewed − full-text excluded = included**. Preserve reasons for exclusions in the screening log. These are screening counts; appraisal can subsequently exclude evidence of inadequate quality. | **Chapter 7, pp. 112–113; Appendix C, pp. 220, 223.** |

**Reading order:** Appendix C, pp. 218–223 → Chapter 7, pp. 93–113 → Chapter 8 and Appendix E1 when Section II calls for appraisal. Confirm that Section I matches the current Appendix B question; the Section II decisions follow the completed E1 appraisals; and the search was recorded on the **targeted** or **exhaustive** route actually taken. On an exhaustive route, confirm that search dates, database-specific strings, counts, screening methods, and flow-chart arithmetic agree with the underlying search and screening logs. Appendix C contains its form on pp. 218–220 and field-by-field instructions on pp. 221–223.

### Process for following Appendix D

Apply the decision tree **to each source individually**. The steps below turn its branches into a working sequence; they are not numbered this way in the textbook.

| Step | What to do | Where to read |

| **2. Start at Appendix D's first decision** | Determine whether the source is a clinical practice guideline, literature review, or evidence summary. If yes, follow the tree on p. 226. | **3. Consult supplemental methods if necessary. | **4. ### Writing check

Confirm that the subtype and checklist match the source; Section I was completed **only on the independent-support route**; dates describe the age of the *underlying evidence*, not simply the source's publication date; and every consequential negative or unclear appraisal answer has a traceable rationale. The quality decision should agree with the checklist, and any first-page recommendations should relate directly to the current EBP question. Distinguish **suitability**, **quality adequacy**, and **level of support** in the project record.

**Quality decision across all three tools:** The fifth edition calls for a reasoned **adequate or inadequate** decision. It does not assign the earlier A/B/C quality grades or use a simple count of “yes” answers as the decision rule (Ch. 8, pp. 121–123).

G1 Section I contains **suitable, adequately appraised pre-appraised evidence**. Extract question-relevant findings and measures** | G2: **Findings that help answer the EBP question; Measures used**. | In **Findings**, summarize the source's specific results, recommendations, or reported observations that address the question. Include direction, comparison, outcome and useful numerical results when the source reports them; mention null or conflicting findings where relevant. Attribute an opinion or case observation as such, and avoid presenting an association as a proven intervention effect. In **Measures used**, name the instruments, outcome definitions, data sources, or qualitative methods the authors used to assess the result; **do not repeat the result in this column**. Example: “Findings: `[reported difference or theme and context]`; Measures: `[named scale, audit indicator, interview method, or data source]`.” If a source has no formal measures, write **“No formal measure reported”** rather than supplying one. | **Chapter 9, pp. 134–135; Appendix G2, pp. 272–273.** |
| **6. Document limitations and support** | G2: **Limitations; Level of support for decision-making; Notes to the team**. | In **Limitations**, combine relevant author-reported caveats with material appraisal concerns, such as selection bias, confounding, missing outcomes, attrition, incomplete methods, or limited transferability. Explain the likely effect on interpreting this particular finding; note that a design-inherent limitation need not by itself make the source inadequate. In **Level of support**, record the category justified by Appendix D and the appraisal: an adequately appraised pre-appraised source that answers only part of the question may offer **strong** support; formal single studies offer **strong** or **moderate** support according to design; and adequate anecdotal evidence remains **limited**. An adequate-quality decision does **not** by itself raise the support level. If fully suitable pre-appraised evidence provides **independent** support, check whether the G1 best-evidence pathway applies instead. In **Notes to the team**, add issues helpful during synthesis that do not fit elsewhere, such as overlapping data, a subgroup or setting mismatch, or a contradiction needing review. Example: “Potential confounding by `[factor]`; findings apply only to `[subgroup]`; compare with article `[number]`.” | **Chapter 8, pp. 120–129; Appendix D, pp. 226–227; Appendices E1–E3, pp. 230–247; Appendix G2, pp. 272–273.** |
| **7. Check the table and move to synthesis** | Final G2 row audit; prepare **Appendix H, Sections I and II**. | Compare every row with its full source and appraisal: ensure all included sources have one complete row, numbers and reviewer names match project records, findings address the current EBP question, **measures are distinct from results**, limitations are accurate, and support levels are justified. Resolve disagreement before finalizing. Reconcile G2 inclusions with the Appendix C screening count **and any later quality-based exclusions**, keeping the reasons for changes. Then group rows by support level and compare findings across sources for **Appendix H Section I**; use the resulting synthesis to write question-answering recommendations and their certainty **in Section II**. Do not substitute a row's individual finding for the team's cross-source synthesis. | **Chapter 9, pp. 134–135, 137–145; Appendix G2, pp. 272–273; Appendix H, pp. 278–282.** |

#### Writing check

Confirm that the documented search was exhaustive and every G2 source was eligible and adequately appraised. Each row identifies its reviewer(s), describes the **actual** source design and sample, extracts only question-relevant findings, distinguishes measures from findings, reports meaningful limitations, and assigns support for decision-making separately from quality adequacy. Keep excluded-source decisions in the project log; use the retained rows for Appendix H synthesis and recommendations.

In both forms, the summary is a **faithful extraction of what the sources report**. The comparison of findings across sources and the team's resulting recommendations occur in the subsequent synthesis step (Ch. 9, pp. 134–135).
